Core Aerodynamic Vocabulary (18 Key Operational Terms)

01. Lift /lɪft/
Upward aerodynamic force perpendicular to the relative wind.
02. Weight (Gravity) /weɪt/
Downward gravitational force acting through the center of gravity (CG).
03. Thrust /θrʌst/
Forward propulsive force produced by engines overcoming drag.
04. Drag /dræɡ/
Rearward retarding force opposing aircraft motion through air.
05. Induced Drag /ɪnˈdjuːst dræɡ/
Drag generated as an unavoidable byproduct of lift generation.
06. Parasitic Drag /ˌpærəˈsɪt.ɪk dræɡ/
Skin friction, form, and interference drag increasing with airspeed.
07. Camber /ˈkæm.bər/
The asymmetry between the upper and lower curves of an airfoil.
08. Chord Line /kɔːd laɪn/
Imaginary straight reference line connecting leading edge to trailing edge.
09. Angle of Attack (AoA) /ˈæŋ.ɡəl əv əˈtæk/
Angle between the chord line and the oncoming relative airflow.
10. Pitch Attitude /pɪtʃ ˈæt.ɪ.tʃuːd/
Angle of aircraft longitudinal axis relative to the natural horizon.
11. Critical Angle of Attack /ˈkrɪt.ɪ.kəl əv əˈtæk/
AoA where airflow detaches from upper surface, resulting in a stall.
12. Boundary Layer /ˈbaʊn.dər.i ˈleɪ.ər/
Thin layer of air directly adjacent to the airfoil surface.
13. Aileron /ˈeɪ.lə.rɒn/
Hinged surface on outer trailing edge controlling roll (longitudinal).
14. Elevator /ˈel.ɪ.veɪ.tər/
Horizontal tail control surface controlling pitch (lateral axis).
15. Rudder /ˈrʌd.ər/
Vertical tail control surface controlling yaw (vertical axis).
16. Center of Gravity (CG) /ˈsen.tər əv ˈɡræv.ɪ.ti/
Average location of the weight of the aircraft.
17. Center of Pressure (CP) /ˈsen.tər əv ˈpreʃ.ər/
Point along chord line where total aerodynamic lift is concentrated.
18. Stall /stɔːl/
Sudden reduction in lift caused by airflow separation above critical AoA.

Secondary Flight Controls & High-Lift Devices

Trailing Edge Flaps:

Extend aft and downward to increase wing camber and surface area, increasing lift coefficient ($C_L$) and drag for low-speed takeoff and landing.

Leading Edge Slats:

Deploy forward to energize the boundary layer through high-energy airflow slots, delaying boundary layer separation to higher angles of attack.

Flight Spoilers & Speedbrakes:

Hinged upper-wing panels that deploy symmetrically to dump aerodynamic lift and increase drag for rapid descent or runway braking deceleration.

Part 3 (Cont.): Language Focus — Conditionals & Cause-and-Effect in Flight Physics

Technical English relies on precise conditional formulas to explain aerodynamic invariants and operational consequences. Avoid vague phrasing.

Zero Conditional: Scientific & Aerodynamic Invariants

Formula: If / When + Present Simple, ... Present Simple

• "If angle of attack exceeds 16 degrees, the boundary layer separates."
• "When airspeed doubles, parasitic drag quadruples."

First Conditional: Operational Causes & Consequences

Formula: If / Unless + Present Simple, ... Will / Can / Must + Verb

• "Unless the pilot pushes the nose forward, the aircraft will stall."
• "If we deploy speedbrakes, the aircraft will decelerate rapidly."

Cause & Effect Connecting Words in Technical Explanations

Connector Pattern Grammatical Structure Aviation Example
results in / leads to Verb + Noun Phrase / Gerund "Increasing wing camber results in higher aerodynamic lift and additional drag."
is caused by / is due to Passive + Noun Phrase "Wingtip vortices are caused by the pressure differential between wing surfaces."
in order to / so as to Conjunction + Infinitive "Slats deploy automatically in order to delay airflow detachment."
whereas / in contrast to Comparative Conjunction "Parasitic drag surges at high speed, whereas induced drag dominates at low speed."

Part 5: The Lift Masterclass & High-Altitude Stall Recovery Simulation

Deliver a 90-second spoken explanation of aerodynamic lift for a non-technical passenger or trainee controller. Combine Bernoulli and Newton.

Model 90-Second Spoken Presentation Script

"Generating aerodynamic lift requires two complementary physical mechanisms. First, an airplane wing has a curved upper camber. When relative wind approaches the leading edge, air flowing over the curved top must accelerate compared to the flatter lower surface. According to Bernoulli’s principle, faster-moving air creates lower static pressure. This pressure difference creates a net upward suction force.

Second, as the wing travels forward with a positive angle of attack, the lower surface deflects oncoming air downward. In accordance with Newton’s third law of motion, for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: deflecting thousands of tons of air downward results in an equal upward force on the aircraft. Together, pressure differentials and downwash produce the total lift needed to overcome the aircraft's weight."

A twin-engine commercial airliner is in level cruise at Flight Level 370 (37,000 ft) over mountain terrain. The aircraft encounters severe clear-air turbulence and a sudden 30-knot headwind loss. Autothrottles advance to maximum continuous thrust, but airspeed continues decaying from Mach 0.78 to Mach 0.71. The autopilot trims nose-up to maintain assigned altitude. Role Card A: First Officer (Pilot Flying)

1. Call out the tactile stick-shaker stall warning immediately.
2. Disconnect the autopilot and autothrottle.
3. Apply forward sidestick pressure to push the nose down below the horizon.
4. Confirm that the angle of attack decreases and buffet ceases.

Standard Callout: "STALL! DISCONNECTING AUTOPILOT! PUSHING NOSE DOWN!"
Role Card B: Captain (Pilot Monitoring)

1. Monitor pitch attitude: ensure FO does not pull aft.
2. Verify roll wings level using rudder pedals, not excessive ailerons.
3. Advance thrust smoothly once nose is down and AoA is reduced.
4. Call out airspeed recovery and notify ATC of descent.

Standard Callout: "PITCH NOSE DOWN CONFIRMED. WINGS LEVEL. AIRSPEED INCREASING."
